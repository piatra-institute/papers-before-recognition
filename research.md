# Research

## What the seed proposed, and what was cut

The seed proposed a 13,000 to 16,000 word paper with thirteen sections, a field name, seven named principles, and three formal models: an "agentic alterity plane" pairing an agency-evidence axis with an interventional translation deficit, a six-term encounter functional over risk, harm, domination, irreversibility and information gain, and a scored formula for activating legal standing.

Most of that apparatus was cut, and the reasons are worth recording because they are the same reasons a reviewer would give.

The six-term functional prices harm, domination, and irreversibility on a common scale and then optimizes. Nothing in the paper justifies the exchange rates, and an optimization whose weights are unargued is a way of hiding a normative decision inside a formula. What survives is the qualitative claim the functional was meant to carry: that information gain, harm, option-space loss, and irreversibility are separate quantities that trade against each other, which the simulation shows by measuring them separately and refusing to add them up except where the addition is stated.

The translation-deficit measure needs a library of familiar agent models, a space of admissible translations between ontologies, and a description-length penalty on translations. Each is a research programme. Stated as a formula it looks like a result and is a promissory note.

The standing formula multiplies power, irreversibility, and expected harm. That much is kept, in words, as the three quantities whose product activates standing, because the structure is doing real work and the false precision of a number is not.

The seed also proposed the field name "xenial ethics" and seven principle names. The field name is kept. Most of the principle names are dropped: a paper that arrives with seven of its own coinages is asking the reader to learn a vocabulary before it has earned one. Two are kept because they name things the paper measures, the recognition hazard and legibility injustice, and one because it is the juridical proposal, xenial standing.

## The corpus constraint

The seed built its empirical section directly on a measurement framework from elsewhere in this institute's corpus, including its notation. Papers here stand alone and cite no sibling paper, so the machinery was re-derived in this paper's own terms and claimed only for what it does here.

The substantive consequence is that the evidence-against-realization gap enters as a normative diagnosis rather than as a contribution to attribution theory. The paper states the gap in words, notes that both quantities are definable interventionally, and moves immediately to the ethical consequence, which is legibility injustice. It makes no claim to novelty in how the attribution is computed. The simulation measures the injustice directly, through policies acting on legibility against policies acting on probes, which needs no attribution machinery at all.

## What the simulation had to be

A philosophy paper with a simulation attached is usually worse than either alone, so the simulation had to earn its place by being able to come out against the argument. Three things it can refute:

1. that a constrained policy costs the host something (it does not: the constrained policy is cheapest of the three);
2. that reading surfaces is merely noisy (it is not: the allocation inverts);
3. that inquiry before irreversible action always pays (it does not: there is a corner where it fails, and a stopping rule is required for it to pay at all).

The third was the most useful result and it was not anticipated. The first version of the xenial policy had no stopping rule, and because it refuses the measurement that would resolve valence it never resolves, so it probed until the round limit and paid for every round. It lost to the surface policy in 11 of 15 regimes. Adding a rule that halts inquiry when the last probe bought less than 0.05 nats took it to 14 of 15. That is not a tuning fix; it is the finding that patience without a stopping rule is paralysis, and it is now a measured result reported beside the dominance claim.

## Modelling choices that carry the argument

**Valence is reachable only destructively.** Observation reports legibility, a reversible probe reports capacity and pushback, and only the destructive probe reports valence. This is the recognition hazard built into the instrument. It is a stipulation, it is the single most load-bearing choice in the model, and the paper says so in the section that introduces the game rather than in a footnote. If valence were cheaply observable the paper's central problem would not arise, which is precisely why the case where it is not is the case worth modelling.

**The legibility gap is designed wide.** Scripted systems sit at 0.88 legibility with 0.10 capacity; valenced systems at 0.18 with 0.66. A threshold policy applied to that gap separates them cleanly, which is why Figure 2 shows 1.0 and 1.0. The paper states that this result is built rather than found, and claims only the size and sign of the failure under stated conditions.

**Harm counts only where there is valence.** Harm to a candidate is the act's harm coefficient multiplied by the candidate's valence, so acts against non-valenced systems cost nothing on that axis. The host never knows valence in advance, which is the difficulty the whole paper is about.

## Sources: what was verified and what was dropped

Every citation was checked against Crossref or OpenLibrary before use. Verified and cited: Levin's TAME framework, the xenobot and anthrobot papers, Lavazza and Massimini on organoid consciousness assessment, Kreitmair on organoid research ethics, Birch on the precautionary principle and the 2024 book, Kurki on legal personhood, Friston and Frith, and the six books (Derrida, Glissant, Waldenfels, Uexkull, Levinas, Negarestani).

Dropped from the seed's list: the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, which is a declaration rather than a peer-reviewed source and adds nothing the Birch citations do not carry; Sloman's space of possible minds, which is real and relevant but was cited in the seed to an archive URL and is not load-bearing for any claim here; Haraway and Deleuze and Guattari, which the seed listed and which the paper never needed, since assemblage claims here are carried by the boundary-hearing argument rather than by assemblage theory; Klyubin and Polani on empowerment, verified but ultimately unused once the option-space measure was defined directly in the simulation rather than through channel capacity.

Lacan and Heidegger appear in the text by name and argument without citation to a specific work, because what the paper uses in each case is a commonplace of the secondary literature rather than a specific passage, and a citation would imply a closer reading than the paper performs.
